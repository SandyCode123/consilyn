How expert network pay works: rates, minutes and payment terms

You set an hourly rate, and the network pays it for the minutes you actually spend with the client. Booked time that goes unused, preparation and calls that never happen are normally unpaid. You then request payment yourself, within a deadline, and the money arrives a few weeks later.

The details differ between networks, and they decide how much of a booked hour you are paid for. This guide sets them out from each network's own terms and expert pages, read on 16 October 2026. Terms change, so check the version you signed. It is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Who sets your rate?

You do, within reason. Every network here lets you name an hourly rate and change it later, but you change it by asking, not by editing a field, and a higher rate can mean fewer calls.

  • GLG says on its page for experts that you set your own rate. To change it, its support page says to contact the Member Success team.
  • Guidepoint's advisor terms pay your customary consulting fee as agreed with Guidepoint. A change needs written notice, takes effect once Guidepoint confirms it in writing, and applies only to future projects. You may not tell clients your rate without Guidepoint's written consent.
  • AlphaSights' page for experts says to discuss your rate with its team when it next contacts you about a project, and that high rates typically lead to lower demand for your expertise.
  • Tegus, now part of AlphaSense, says on its page for experts that you set rates reflecting your expertise and experience.

How much do experts charge?

None of these networks' terms gives a typical rate. One network that does publish ranges is Arbolus, in How much do expert networks pay?: roughly $100 to $300 an hour for most experts, $300 to $800 for senior or highly specialised ones, $800 to over $1,000 for rare expertise, and a fixed fee, often $40 to $150, for surveys. Treat those as one network's view, not a market survey.

The client pays the network more than the network pays you. Silverlight Research, another network, says in how expert network pricing is structured that clients' rates commonly fall between $700 and $1,500 an hour, while experts' fees range from around $200 an hour to $600 or more, the difference being the network's margin. Your rate is what you receive, not what the client is charged.

Which minutes are you paid for?

The minutes you spend with the client, not the slot that was booked. The client decides when the call ends, so an hour booked can be a forty-minute call. How part-minutes and short overruns count differs, and over many calls it adds up.

  • GLG: the total minutes on the call with clients, as GLG's systems record them, with partial minutes rounded down (GLG member terms, last updated April 2022).
  • Guidepoint: prorated to the number of full minutes completed. Its terms also say the client decides how long a consultation lasts, which may differ from the time scheduled.
  • Third Bridge: prorated by the minutes spent with the client, with payment rounded to the nearest whole number (Third Bridge expert terms, clause 7.2, last revised December 2024).
  • AlphaSights: your agreed hourly rate, pro rata for the time you spend interacting with clients.
  • Tegus: prorated and rounded up to the nearest 15 minutes, with a 5-minute grace period. A call that runs less than 5 minutes past a 15-minute mark is rounded down; 5 minutes or more is rounded up.

What is not paid?

Preparation, waiting, and calls that do not happen. That holds even for a call cancelled at short notice. Where a network sets a limit on preparation, keep to it unless it approves more.

  • Preparation. GLG's terms say you are paid only for time spent interacting with clients, and not for preparation time, wait time or time set aside for a project that does not happen, unless GLG agrees otherwise in writing or the project invitation says so. Guidepoint's terms say to do no more than 15 minutes of preparatory work for a project without its explicit approval.
  • Cancellations and no-shows. Guidepoint does not pay for consultations that do not take place, even if they were scheduled. Third Bridge owes nothing for consultations that do not take place, "for any reason" (clause 7.2). AlphaSights says clients occasionally reschedule or cancel, and that it tells you as soon as it can.
  • Screening questions. None of these terms pays for answering them. See screening questions that ask for free consulting.

What if you end a call to stay within the rules?

You are usually still paid, which is deliberate: networks want you to stop the moment a call heads somewhere you should not go. Tell the network straight away.

GLG's terms say that if you stop a project to comply with them and promptly tell GLG, you may request payment for the full time you set aside. Guidepoint's say you may request payment for a full project you end early to comply with them, and that you must tell it by email straight away. Third Bridge's clause 1.3 pays the time you actually spent plus 15 minutes when you have to end a consultation early because of a professional, regulatory or ethical conflict. For what to watch for on a call, see how to prepare for an expert network call.

How and when do you get paid?

You ask for it. Each network has a deadline for requesting payment and a usual time to pay after that. Miss the request deadline and you may wait longer, or have to chase the payment.

  • GLG: request payment through its website within 30 days of completing a project, and complete any attestations the project requires first.
  • Guidepoint: submit your invoice within 45 days of completing a project, through its website. Payment typically follows within 15 days.
  • Third Bridge: confirm within two business days that the consultation took place, then apply for payment in its web app. It pays within 30 days of the later of your payment request or the client's confirmation. Report a missing payment by email within six months of the end of that 30 days.
  • AlphaSights: its standard payment terms are 30 days. You receive a secure link for a bank transfer after the call, and experts in the US and Canada can ask for a cheque.
  • Tegus: payments go through Tipalti, within 15 business days of the call, in US dollars.

Payment can also be withheld for avoidable reasons. Third Bridge's terms list not reporting a consultation, arranging one directly with a client, and bank details that do not match the name on your account. GLG's say payment depends on any post-project attestation. Guidepoint's let it withhold fees if you have not completed its advisor tutorial in time.

Who handles tax?

You do, almost always. Most networks pay you a gross fee and leave tax to you. What they report, and from which country they pay, varies. This is general information, not tax advice: ask an adviser where you live.

  • GLG: your contract is with Gerson Lehrman Group, Inc., a US company, and you are responsible for any tax due where you are. GLG may ask for a tax identification number.
  • Guidepoint: asks for your tax identification number, provides a US Form 1099 where required, and says you are responsible for any tax due.
  • AlphaSights: is incorporated in the UK, so it does not issue 1099s to experts in the US. It says to follow your own country's rules for income from the UK.
  • Third Bridge: deducts any withholding taxes, and any charges from the bank you name, from the fee (clause 7.2).

What if your employer has rules about outside pay?

Follow them, and tell the network, because several can pay in other ways. GLG's terms say that if you are employed you must follow your employer's policies on payment from GLG, and that if you or your employer ask for payments to go to your employer, GLG pays them there. Guidepoint's say its payment system can follow an employer's guidelines, including joint payment, payment to an institution, or a donation to a charity you choose. AlphaSights also offers a donation to its charity partner instead of a fee.

A short checklist for getting paid

  1. Know your network's request deadline: 30 days at GLG, 45 at Guidepoint, two business days to confirm at Third Bridge.
  2. Note each call's real length the day it happens, so you can check the payment.
  3. Complete any attestation the project asks for. At GLG, payment depends on it.
  4. Keep your bank details current and in the same name as your account.
  5. Never arrange work with a client directly. Third Bridge's terms let it withhold payment for that, and GLG's bar soliciting a client it introduced you to for one year.
  6. Keep preparation within the network's limit, unless it agrees to more.

How Consilyn fits

Consilyn has nothing to do with payments. It is a live answer screen for the call itself, in your browser on Windows and Mac. On calls where it is allowed and the client has agreed, it transcribes the call on your laptop, never saving the audio, and shows short answers to read aloud, built from your screening answers and background. Every AI-written answer is marked not verified; where it uses your own words, a green label names the source and quotes them.

The call's text and your notes go to Google Gemini using your own key, and on Gemini's free tier Google may use that text to improve its products and people may review it. Consilyn does not decide whether a call allows it: your network's terms and your client do, so check your network's current terms before you use it.
