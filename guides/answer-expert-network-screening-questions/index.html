How to answer expert network screening questions

Screening questions are short and easy to rush. A few lines typed between meetings decide whether a client picks you, what they ask you for an hour, and how much of it you can answer with confidence. Here is a simple way to write them well. Where it describes networks' rules, this guide is general information, not legal advice.

What are screening questions for, and who reads them?

Screening questions let a client choose between experts before paying for anyone's time. The network typically sends a shortlist with each expert's answers, and the client picks the people whose answers show direct, relevant experience. Your answers are, in effect, your pitch for the call and the client's first briefing on you.

The network's own team reads them first, to check you fit the project. Then the client reads them, often beside several other experts' answers to the same questions. GLG's guidance for its experts describes the same order: a project manager selects you for your expertise, and the client then reviews your credentials and agrees to speak with you.

That reader is short of time and looking for one thing: whether you have seen this from the inside, recently enough to matter.

What structure works best?

Answer the question first, then give the first-hand basis for your answer, then say where your knowledge stops. Three or four sentences is usually enough. The structure works because it gives the client exactly what they need to decide: a yes or no, the evidence behind it, and an honest boundary.

  1. The direct answer. "Yes", "partly" or "not directly, but" in the first few words. Do not make the reader dig for it.
  2. The first-hand basis. Your role, the years, the scale (budget, sites, team, volumes) and one concrete detail only someone who did the work would know.
  3. Where your knowledge stops. A sentence on what you did not do, or know only second-hand. It reads as confidence, not weakness, and it stops the call drifting into ground you cannot cover.

What does a good answer look like?

A good answer is specific, recent and bounded. The weak versions below are not dishonest; they are vague, and vague answers lose to specific ones. All three examples, including every figure in them, are invented to show the shape, and the same pattern works in any field you are screened for.

Manufacturing procurement

Question: Describe your experience negotiating with suppliers of aluminium castings.

Before: I have extensive procurement experience across the automotive sector and have negotiated with many suppliers. I am very familiar with aluminium pricing and market dynamics and can speak to all aspects of the supply chain.

After: Yes, directly. From 2016 to 2022 I was category manager for castings at a mid-sized maker of commercial-vehicle parts, buying around 60 million euros a year from nine suppliers. I led the annual price rounds and moved our contracts onto an alloy-index formula in 2019. I have never worked on the supplier side, so I know their margins only from what they told us across the table.

Hospital purchasing

Question: How do hospitals evaluate and buy surgical staplers?

Before: Very familiar. I have worked in healthcare for over twenty years and know the surgical device market extremely well.

After: From the buying side, closely. Until 2024 I spent six years running value analysis for a group of four regional hospitals and chaired the committee that reviewed surgical consumables, staplers included. We ran two stapler tenders in that time; the second cut us from three suppliers to one. I can explain how surgeons, finance and procurement each weigh in. I cannot speak to what manufacturers charge other hospital groups.

Energy

Question: How do industrial sites decide whether to install battery storage?

Before: I am an expert in energy storage with deep knowledge of the industrial battery market, including all major technologies, vendors and trends.

After: I can answer for food manufacturers specifically. As energy manager for a chilled-food producer with five sites, from 2018 to 2023, I wrote the business cases for batteries at two of them. One was approved, a 2 MWh system sized mainly to cut peak demand charges. I know how finance teams in that sector judge payback. Utility-scale storage and battery chemistry are outside my first-hand experience.

Should you aim to sound impressive?

No. Aim to be accurate. Never stretch your title, your years, your scale or how close you were to a decision. Networks can check your background, clients test claims in the first minutes of a call, and every overstatement becomes a question you then have to answer live, with the client listening.

The networks' own rules say this directly. GLG's Code of Conduct requires accurate information about your qualifications and work experience, and says members may not accept projects outside their areas of expertise. AlphaSights' Expert Code of Conduct says experts must not mislead anyone about their relevant expertise, and must give accurate, up-to-date and verifiable career information. Guidepoint's terms for advisers reserve the right, for Guidepoint and its clients, to run background checks and confirm your professional and educational history. Third Bridge's expert terms require the biographical information you give to be up to date, true and not misleading.

How do you keep your figures consistent with the call?

Treat every number in a screening answer as one you will be asked to explain. Give its basis and period, round honestly, prefer a range to false precision, and keep a copy so you can reread it before the call. If you later realise a figure was wrong, correct it yourself at the start of the call.

The client will have written your figures down. If your screening answer said a supplier switch saved "around 8%" and on the call you say "roughly a fifth", the client now doubts both. Most inconsistencies are innocent: a different year, a different base, or a figure remembered more generously under pressure. Prevent them by stating the base in writing ("8% of the castings budget in the first year") and repeating it the same way when asked.

What must you never put in a screening answer?

Share your own experience and judgement, never your employer's confidential information or material non-public information: information that is not generally available and that a reasonable investor would likely consider important to an investment decision. If a question can only be answered with that, say so in your answer and offer what you can share instead.

Each network quoted here says so in its own terms. AlphaSights bars disclosing information specific to a company you currently work for or represent, and any material non-public, confidential or proprietary information. Guidepoint's terms say you must decline or stop any project that would disclose confidential or proprietary information that is not exclusively yours, or material non-public information about a public company or security. GLG's Code says members should never share confidential information with GLG or its clients. Third Bridge bars disclosing confidential information, trade secrets or material non-public information, and counts unreleased margins or revenues as confidential.

In practice, speak about patterns, ranges and how decisions get made, not a company's unreleased numbers, contracts or plans. A line such as "I can describe how these deals are usually structured, but not my current employer's terms" protects you and still helps the client.

This is general information, not legal advice. What binds you is the actual text of your network's terms, your employment contract and any confidentiality agreements you have signed. Read them, and take advice if you are unsure.

Can you use AI to write your screening answers?

Write them yourself, in your own words, from your own experience. Several networks restrict AI-generated content in ways that reach screening answers: GLG's rule names written responses to project opportunities, AlphaSights' plagiarism rule, which covers AI, applies to any vetting process, and Third Bridge bars AI help with any question it or its clients ask. Check your network's current terms before using any AI tool.

GLG's Code of Conduct says that, other than as GLG builds AI into its own systems, members may not use or rely on AI-generated content in phone consultations, surveys, written work or written responses to project opportunities. AlphaSights' code says that presenting AI-produced insights as your own is plagiarism, and prohibits using information directly and exclusively from AI tools such as chatbots. Guidepoint's terms bar misrepresenting your experience "through the use of artificial intelligence or otherwise", and say advisers may not record or transcribe consultations, including through AI tools. Third Bridge's Expert Code of Conduct bars using any form of AI to answer, or help answer, any question from Third Bridge or its clients, which includes its screening questions. For more, see what the rules say about AI, and for the call itself, how to prepare for an expert network call.

Why keep a private library of your past answers?

Keep a private record of what you submit: the date, the topic and your exact wording. It saves time on the next project, keeps your account of your career consistent across many clients, and lets you reread precisely what a client read about you before the call starts.

Keep it private, and check what your network's terms let you keep. Guidepoint's terms count a client's questions and the topics discussed as confidential information, not to be used for any purpose the terms do not permit, and Third Bridge's terms say much the same of a consultation's subject matter and questions. So store the record on your own device, describe topics in general words rather than copying the client's question or naming the client, and never share it.

Use it as raw material, not paste-in paragraphs: a reused block that half-answers a new question reads exactly like one.

Why do your screening answers frame the whole call?

Because they are what the client chose you on, they are what the client prepares from. Expect the first questions to start from your own words ("you mentioned the 2019 index change") and the call to stay inside the scope you described. Write each answer as the opening of a conversation you can actually hold.

This is also why preparation can be light. GLG's guidance says your first-hand knowledge is all you need to bring and that there is typically no need to prepare, and Guidepoint's terms ask advisers to do no more than 15 minutes of preparatory work for a project without its approval. Rereading your own screening answers fits easily in that time, and reminds you of the frame you set, the figures you gave and where you said your knowledge stops.
