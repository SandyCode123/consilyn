A client has read your profile and screening answers and booked a paid consultation. Here is what happens on the call, what comes up, what to keep to yourself, and how to set up.

What does a typical expert network call look like?

Most calls are a phone or video conversation booked for a set slot. The client leads, working through their own questions, and you answer from your own experience. The client decides when to stop, and networks typically pay for the minutes you actually spend on the call rather than the time booked.

GLG describes its calls as led by the client and as "a discussion of your direct knowledge and experience" (GLG Expert Support). The GLG, Guidepoint and Third Bridge terms linked below all pay by the minute.

The usual shape is a short introduction, a few minutes confirming your background and how close you were to the topic, then the client's questions and follow-ups. Near the end, some clients ask what they should have asked, or what kind of person to speak to next.

Both GLG's membership terms and Guidepoint's advisor terms say someone may listen in for compliance purposes without announcing themselves. Speak as if the call is being reviewed, because it may be.

How much preparation do networks expect?

Less than you might think. Networks select you for what you already know, and GLG says there is typically no need to prepare. Useful preparation is short: re-read your screening answers, refresh the facts of your own roles, decide what you cannot discuss, and check your setup. Researching the client's subject from scratch is not the job.

GLG adds that your project manager will tell you if a client wants specific preparation. Guidepoint's terms say advisers should do no more than 15 minutes of preparatory work without its explicit approval, and GLG's terms say preparation time is not paid unless agreed otherwise.

Why re-read your screening answers before the call?

Because the client chose you partly on them and will often start there. Your screening answers are a record of what you said you know, and the call should match them. If you now remember a date, a figure or a role differently, say so early and plainly rather than letting the conversation drift away from what you wrote.

An invented example: you wrote that you ran packaging procurement for a mid-sized food manufacturer from 2019 to 2023. Expect the first question to be about exactly that: how many suppliers, how the tender ran, what moved the price. An answer that widens your remit or blurs the dates costs you credibility in the first five minutes.

Keep your own copy of everything you submit.

What questions do clients ask on expert calls?

Most questions fall into a handful of groups: your role and how close you were to decisions, your view of the market, competitors, pricing, how buyers choose, and what you expect next. Clients also ask what they should ask others, which tests whether you understand the topic beyond your own seat.

  • Your role. What you did, what budget or volume you handled, and which decisions were yours.
  • Market view. What is growing or shrinking, and what is driving it.
  • Competitors. Who wins business and why, and who is losing ground.
  • Pricing. How pricing is usually structured and what makes customers accept an increase or switch. Answer in general patterns, not with your employer's actual contract terms.
  • Buying decisions. Who signs off, on what criteria, and how long it takes. A former warehouse operations director might be asked how mid-sized retailers choose a logistics provider.
  • What comes next. Where you expect the market to go, and what would change your mind.
  • What would you ask others? Which questions you would put to a competitor, a customer or a regulator, and what kind of person they should speak to next.

How should you answer as the expert you were screened as?

Lead with what you saw first-hand, then give your wider view and label it as a view. When a question goes past what you know, say so plainly. Clients pay for first-hand experience, and a clear "I didn't see that directly" protects the value of everything else you say.

A useful pattern is two layers: "In my last role we did this, because of that", then "More broadly, my read is…". Keep answers short, then let the client steer.

Do not stretch into areas you were not screened for. GLG's code of conduct says members may not misrepresent their backgrounds or accept projects outside their expertise. AlphaSights' expert code of conduct says you must not mislead anyone about your expertise, and prohibits passing off AI-produced insights as your own or using information taken directly and exclusively from AI tools.

Do not give investment advice, such as whether a share is worth buying: GLG's and Guidepoint's terms both prohibit it.

What must you not share on an expert call?

Never share confidential information from a current or former employer, or material non-public information about any company. In practice that means internal figures, unreleased results, contract terms, customer details given in confidence, plans and trade secrets. Offer patterns, publicly known facts and your own judgement instead.

Third Bridge's expert terms define material non-public information as information that is not generally available and that a reasonable investor would be substantially likely to consider important to an investment decision. Its examples include results before publication, non-public details of a merger or major contract, and unpublished clinical trial data.

Networks also restrict calls about your current employer. GLG's and Guidepoint's terms both bar projects about a company you currently work for, and calls for a client that competes directly with it; GLG's allow these only with written consent from the company and GLG.

If a question heads somewhere you cannot go, say so calmly: "I can't go into that company's numbers, but I can tell you how buyers generally think about it." If the questions make you uncomfortable, GLG's code says to end the project and contact GLG immediately.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your employment contract, any non-disclosure agreements and your network's current terms decide what applies to you, so check them.

What compliance attestations do networks ask for?

Expect three kinds: a compliance tutorial before you start and at intervals after, confirmations that no conflict or contract stops you taking part, and sometimes an attestation afterwards that you shared nothing confidential. Some networks also set rules on recording calls and on using AI.

  • A tutorial. GLG's terms require its Network Member tutorial before you take part in projects other than surveys, repeated each year. Guidepoint's require its Advisor Compliance Tutorial at least once in the 12 months before each project.
  • Confirmations. GLG's terms have you confirm, when you sign them, that no contract or employer policy prevents you taking part, and say clients may ask for further confirmations as a condition of a project.
  • An attestation afterwards. GLG's terms say you may be asked to attest that you disclosed nothing confidential, breached no duty to an employer and will not use what you learned from the client, and that invoicing a project counts as that attestation.
  • Recording and AI. GLG's terms say members will not record or transcribe their consultations, and its code says members may not use or rely on AI-generated content during phone consultations, other than as GLG itself provides. Guidepoint's terms do not permit advisers to record or transcribe consultations through AI tools, personal devices or other methods. Third Bridge's Expert Code of Conduct bars using any form of AI to answer, or help answer, any question from Third Bridge or its clients. What the rules say covers each network in more detail.

Again, this is general information, not legal advice: read your network's current terms before using any note-taking or assistant tool on a call.

How should you set up for the call?

Treat it like a client meeting you cannot reschedule: a quiet room, a reliable headset, a laptop on mains power and a call link you have already tested. Small failures, such as a dropped connection or a muffled microphone, cost minutes you are paid for and some of the client's patience.

  • A quiet room, door closed and notifications off.
  • A headset you have used on calls before. Bluetooth headsets can switch modes when a call starts, so wired is more predictable.
  • Mains power. A laptop on battery may slow itself down, and a video call feels it.
  • The link, tested a day ahead, with the call app updated.
  • Your notes. Your screening answers and a few lines on each relevant role.

What should you check before the call starts?

Run through this list about half an hour before. It takes ten minutes and covers the things that tend to go wrong: forgetting what you wrote, being unsure what is off limits, and the technology failing at the first question.

  1. Re-read your screening answers and note anything you would now correct.
  2. Write down what you cannot discuss.
  3. Check the call is not about your current employer or for its direct competitor. If it is, contact the network before you join.
  4. Pick two or three first-hand examples you can describe without confidential detail.
  5. Decide how you will say "that is outside what I saw directly".
  6. Plug the laptop in and close apps that compete for the microphone or connection.
  7. Test the headset microphone inside the call app.
  8. Check the time zone on the invitation, and keep the network's contact details to hand.
  9. Join a few minutes early.

What should you do after the call?

Complete any attestation honestly, report anything that worried you, and submit your payment request on time. Do not contact the client directly or post about the project. Then take five minutes to note the questions you were asked: they show what the next client is likely to want.

If a client pressed for confidential information, tell the network even if you declined. GLG's code says members should not communicate with clients without GLG's written approval or describe a project on any public forum, including social media. Payment requests have deadlines: 30 days after a project under GLG's terms, 45 under Guidepoint's. Update your profile if your role or restrictions change; each network above requires it.

How Consilyn fits

Consilyn is a Windows app for experts. It transcribes the call on your laptop, never saving the audio, and shows short answers you can read aloud, built from your own screening answers and background. Every AI-written answer is marked not verified. Where it draws on your own material, a green label names the source and your exact words are quoted underneath. It will not start listening until you confirm you have told the client the call is transcribed on your laptop and its text sent to Google's AI, and they are happy to go ahead. It can also prepare answers to likely questions before the call, which involves no call audio, though network rules on AI still apply.

The call text and your material, never the audio, go to Google Gemini using your own key. On Gemini's free tier, Google may use that text to improve its products, and people may review it. GLG's and Guidepoint's terms above forbid experts transcribing consultations, GLG's code bars relying on AI-generated content during calls, and Third Bridge's code bars any AI help in answering questions, so don't use Consilyn for those networks' calls, including answers prepared beforehand to read out on them. Check your own network's terms, and where they forbid it, don't use it. It is a prompt for your own knowledge, not a substitute for it.
