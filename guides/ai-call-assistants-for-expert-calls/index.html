AI assistants for expert calls: Cluely, Otter and others compared

Guide · Updated 16 October 2026

Most AI call tools were built for something other than expert calls. Cluely and Final Round AI suggest answers during job interviews and meetings. Otter, Fireflies and Granola take notes. An expert network call is different in three ways that decide which tool fits, if any: the client is paying for your own first-hand view, your screening answers are already on record, and many networks restrict AI on their calls.

This guide compares what each tool says it does, from its own website, read on 16 October 2026. Products and prices change, so check the linked pages before you choose. Where it describes networks' rules, it is general information, not legal advice.

What kinds of AI call tool are there?

There are three kinds. Live answer tools listen and put a suggested answer on your screen while the other person waits. Note-takers transcribe the meeting and write a summary, sometimes with a chat you can question. Expert call assistants listen too, but build each answer from what you have already submitted for that call and show which parts are yours.

The difference that matters on an expert call is where an answer comes from. A general answer from a model can sound fluent and still not be your experience. That is the thing the client is paying for, and several networks' codes forbid passing it off as your own.

What does each tool do during the call?

Each summary below is taken from the tool's own pages.

  • Cluely. A desktop app that listens to the conversation and answers when you ask it, with a keyboard shortcut. It does not join the meeting as a bot. You can add custom instructions and upload files. Its pricing page lists a free plan with limited answers, Pro at $19.99 a month, and a $149.99-a-month plan that adds being hidden from screen-sharing software, which its undetectability page describes as using Cluely "without a trace".
  • Final Round AI. An interview copilot for job seekers. Its home page says it listens to a live interview and streams a short suggested answer to a private window, with a Stealth Mode, on by default, designed to keep its windows out of supported screen shares and recordings. It runs on macOS 14.4 or later and on Windows. Live sessions need its paid Pro plan.
  • Otter. A transcription and note-taking service. Its pricing page describes a notetaker that joins Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet to write and share notes, desktop apps for Mac and Windows, and live transcription on the free plan, which caps each conversation at 30 minutes.
  • Fireflies. A note-taker that records Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams and other platforms through a bot that joins the meeting, or through a Chrome extension without the bot, according to its pricing page. It lists real-time notes and live transcription.
  • Granola. A note-taker that does not join as a bot. Its home page says it uses your computer's audio and has notes ready when the meeting ends. It runs on macOS, Windows, iOS and Android, and has a free tier.
  • Consilyn. A Windows app for experts on paid consultations. It transcribes both sides on your laptop, never saving the audio, and nothing joins the meeting. When the client finishes a question, it shows a short answer built from your screening answers and background notes. Every AI-written answer is marked not verified; where it uses your own material, a green label names the source and quotes your exact words. It is made by Amaltas Labs, the publisher of this guide.

Why is an expert call different from a job interview or a meeting?

Three things change. The client pays for your experience, not for a well-phrased answer. The client has already read your screening answers and will test the call against them. And the call happens under a network's terms, which may restrict recording, transcription and AI.

Your experience is the product. AlphaSights' Expert Code of Conduct says presenting AI-produced insights as your own is plagiarism, and prohibits using information directly and exclusively from AI tools such as chatbots. A tool that writes fluent general answers works against you here, unless it keeps your own material and the model's guesses visibly apart.

Your screening answers are on record. The client chose you on them and has written your figures down. An answer that says "about a fifth" where your screening answer said "around 8%" costs you more than a pause would. A note-taker cannot help with that during the call; a general answer tool does not know what you wrote.

There is a third party's voice on the line. A live tool has to capture the client's voice to work, which brings in call-recording law and the network's own rules. That is the same whether a bot appears in the meeting or not.

Does it matter whether the tool can be seen?

Not for the rules. A tool that cannot be seen still captures the client's voice, still sends their words to an AI service, and still falls under your network's terms. A network that forbids experts transcribing calls with AI tools forbids it whether or not anyone can tell.

Guidepoint's advisor terms say advisers may not record or transcribe consultations "through the use of AI tools, personal devices, or other methods". GLG's Code of Conduct says members may not use or rely on AI-generated content in phone consultations, other than AI that GLG builds into its own systems. Third Bridge's Expert Code of Conduct bars using any form of AI to answer, or help answer, questions from Third Bridge or its clients. And in places that require everyone on a call to agree to it being recorded, which in the US includes California, Florida, Illinois, Pennsylvania and Washington according to the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, consent is about the capture, not about what shows on screen.

Consilyn takes the opposite approach to hiding. It will not start listening until you confirm you have told the client the call is transcribed on your laptop and its text sent to Google's AI, and that they are happy to go ahead. For what each network's rules say, see can you use AI on an expert network call?

What should you compare before choosing a tool?

Start with whether your network allows any live tool at all. If it does, compare where each answer comes from, what happens to the audio and the text, and whether you can read the answer aloud without stumbling. Features matter less than these.

  1. Allowed at all? Read your network's current terms for recording, transcription and AI.
  2. Built from what? Does it answer from your own submitted material, and does it show which parts are yours and which are the model's?
  3. Consistent with what you wrote? Does anything check an answer against your screening answers before you say it?
  4. What happens to the audio? Is it saved, and where? Is it sent anywhere?
  5. Where does the text go? Which AI provider receives it, and may that provider use it to improve its products or have people read it? Free and paid tiers often differ.
  6. Does it ask about consent? Does it make you confirm the client agreed before it listens, or leave that to memory?
  7. Can you say it? Is each answer short enough to read aloud while the client waits, with the direct answer first?
  8. Does it run where you take calls? Your operating system, your call app and your headset.

Where does Consilyn fit, and where does it not?

It fits an expert taking calls on a Windows laptop, for a network that allows live help, with a client who agrees. It is built around the screening answers you submitted for that call. It is not a meeting note-taker for your own team, and it is not for job interviews.

The limits are worth knowing before you download it. It runs on Windows 10 and 11 only, in English. Answers are written by Google Gemini using your own key: the call's text and your notes go to Google, never the audio, and on Gemini's free tier Google may use that text to improve its products and people may review it. Consilyn does not decide whether a call allows it: your network's terms and your client do, so check your network's current terms before you use it.

If you only need a record of a call your network lets you transcribe, a note-taker may be all you need. If you want answers on screen during an expert call, look for one that tells you plainly which words are yours.
